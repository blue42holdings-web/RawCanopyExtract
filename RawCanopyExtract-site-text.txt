THE BEST FORM FOR EACH
HEADING: Fruiting body where it counts. Mycelium where the research is.
Most mushroom products pick one form and use it for everything, usually mycelium grown on grain, because it is cheaper. We pick per species. Lion's mane and cordyceps are fruiting body: the mushroom itself, harvested whole and extracted whole, which is also what the human research on lion's mane used.
  LINK: "human research on lion's mane" -> https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/biomedres/40/4/40_125/_article  (opens in a new tab)
Turkey tail is the exception. Its best-known extracts are made from cultured mycelium, and that is what the research on turkey tail and the gut used, so that is the form we use. Whichever the form, the rule doesn't change: one species per packet, no grain passed off as mushroom, beta-glucan measured on every lot.
  LINK: "made from cultured mycelium" -> opens the "Receipts" page (on this site)

HEADING: WHAT IT IS
Trametes versicolor grows in overlapping layers on fallen wood in temperate forests: thin, leathery fans stacked one over the next, each rippled with concentric bands of brown, rust, gray and blue and finished with a wavy white edge. Versicolor means of several colors, and no two fans agree on which. You will also find it under its older name, Coriolus versicolor. The English name is plain description: a banded fan, spread open, really does look like a wild turkey's tail.
In China it is Yún zhī, the cloud mushroom, for the way those bands swirl across the cap. In Japan it is Kawaratake. Its use in China is documented back at least two thousand years, and it was written into the Compendium of Materia Medica during the Ming Dynasty. It is too tough to eat, so it has always been prepared: simmered, steeped, extracted.

BUTTON: "01What is a fruiting body?+"
The mushroom itself: the part that grows above the log. Mycelium is the root-like network underneath it. Most products on the shelf are mycelium grown on rice or oats and milled together with the grain, so the grain is part of what you swallow. Our lion's mane and cordyceps are fruiting body. Our turkey tail is mycelium, because that is the form the research tested, and it is extracted on its own, never milled in with grain. The beta-glucan result is how you tell the difference from the outside.
BUTTON: "02What is beta-glucan and why do you publish it?+"
Beta-glucans are the compounds that make up the mushroom's cell walls, and they are the standard way a laboratory measures how much mushroom is in a mushroom powder. Grain reads low, so the number is hard to fake. We publish it because it is the one figure in this category that can be checked, and because we can't ask you to trust a claim we haven't shown you.

Chapter One
In Orrin, there is a mountain that wears a cloud, and the cloud does not leave it. Three flat disks stacked one upon another, turning slow above the snow, winding down through the air like a screw. Wind comes off the high country and pours straight through it and out the far side and is gone, and still the cloud does not move, and it is not made of the same air twice in all a day.
The old people said the mountain was breathing. The young ones said it was only weather. No one who lives beneath it has ever thought this strange. There has never been a morning without it.
Inside, it was morning also, though morning inside a mountain is a thing men agree upon rather than a thing they see.
High on the fourth terrace, a woman leaned out over the rail and shook a blanket, and the dust came down through the crystal light in a long bright drift, and took the better part of a minute to reach the floor.
Below her, the great gallery of Orrin rose in terraces of cut stone, stairs folding back upon themselves, doors stacked six deep, bridges thrown across the gap at four heights, every foot of it lit by clear crystal set into the walls.
Their grandfathers’ grandfathers had come up out of the water with nothing but each other, and had found these caverns already cut and waiting, and had never learned by whom. This was the whole of the world, and it was enough, and no one had ever wanted for more.
At the foot of the west stair, the crew was waiting with their picks and their coiled rope, and they looked up the stair and they looked at one another.
“He is asleep, as he was yesterday and as he will be tomorrow. I will fetch him.”
Somebody laughed, and the crew settled in to wait, as they have waited many times before.
Three flights up and along a passage was a room cut square from the rock, and in it a man flat on his back, with his legs thrown wide, one bare leg escaped from the sheet and hanging free, and a pillow pulled down over his eyes. Both hands holding it fast against the morning.
Raken was snoring beneath it. Politely, wholeheartedly, and it filled the room entire.
Adimu stood in the doorway and looked upon him with great affection and no patience whatsoever. Then he yanked the pillow from his face.
Raken woke as a man comes up out of deep water. Eyes wide, hair flat on the one side and standing upon the other, blinking at a room far brighter than he had agreed to. He was young and smooth of face, and his was the sort of face that has not yet been asked to do anything hard.
“Wake up, let’s go. Everyone is waiting. And you can carry the crate, sleepyhead.”
So he came out of his room, with the crate in his arms and his coat half on and one boot not properly upon his foot, and he went down the last flights at a run.
Past the second landing there were women working at the water channel, where the cold stream came down and ran along the wall in its cut course. And the eldest knelt at the edge of it, with her sleeves shoved past the elbow, and Xiomara looked at him coming down the stairs.
“Late again, huh?”
He was very late, and he stopped anyway. She rose to her feet, wiping her hands upon her skirts, waiting to see what he would say. Her two friends had gone quiet in the way that friends go quiet when they intend to hear everything.
Raken put the crate down, and then what he did was bow to her. Deep, and no shame in him at all.
“My lady. I had the finest evening of my life in your company, and I intend to have another. Tonight I mean to build upon it. Trust first, then a house of our own. Then a long and disgraceful old age with too many children in it.”
“You have thought about this a great deal for a man who cannot rise from his bed.”
“I have thought of nothing else.”
Her friends were laughing. Xiomara did not. She looked at him with one eyebrow raised, which in her was the same as laughing. And he knew it, and she knew he knew it. And that was the whole of their courtship, in a single look.
And upon the step above them sat Teo. Xiomara’s brother was all of seven, watching Raken the way a boy watches the only thing in the world he means to become.
Then Raken turned off to work. Coat over the shoulder, and he jumped and struck his heels together in the air and came down in a strut. He called back to her, with a smirk upon his face:
“I will see you tonight. This day will take an eternity.”
And he was gone down the gallery.
Behind him, Teo set his bucket upon the step. He slung his jacket over his shoulder in the very same manner, jumped, struck his heels together badly, landing hard, nearly going over. Grinned a mischievous grin, then ran after Raken down the passage and across the waterfall bridge.
“Where do you think you’re going? Get back here!”
Chapter Two
In Orrin, the working face was a raw place, and it was the whole of their inheritance. Rough stone. Timber props braced floor to ceiling, dust standing in the lamp beams, black water sheeting down one wall and running away in a cut channel. The air was wet and tasted of stone, and the sound of it was the sound of iron on rock, over and over, from four hundred years back to that morning.
For this was the labor here, and the labor of their fathers before them, from the day the water took the old country to that very morning.
Gold lay in the seams. Emerald green glowed wherever the lamp swung by, and silver shined brightly. Easily enough to buy a kingdom some places on earth. They shoveled all of it into the spoil and thought no more about it. For when a man has cut through gold every day of four hundred years, gold is gravel.
They came for the crystal. Clear as light, cold to the hand, glowing faint of its own accord. They cut it free and set it in padded crates, and it went back into the city and into the walls, and the city stayed lit.
Raken was bent to a seam with a wedge in his hand when the mountain spoke. He had the crystal half free, the finest he had cut in a season. He stopped and his face changed.
It was not a shake. Not yet. It was a growl, low and steady in the stone beneath his feet. He held still. He waited for it to be nothing. And then it grew.
He turned and looked up the tunnel toward the mouth of it. Dust was already coming down through the beams. The first stones were bouncing on the floor and skittering away into the dark, and the lamps hung on their nails were swinging all together.
Then the roof let go.
Rock came off the ceiling in sheets. Men were running in every direction and shouting things that no one could hear. And over all of it came Calder’s voice.
“Collapse! Everyone out! It’s happening again!”
Behind a fallen rock, low to the ground, with his hands over his face, terrified, was the boy. There were clean tracks cut through the dust on his cheeks where the tears had run, and his mouth was open, and nothing at all was coming out of it.
Raken saw him, turned, and ran back into the collapsing cave.
“Where are you going?”
Adimu roared from safety, for he had seen nothing, and his friend was running the wrong way into a mountain coming down upon itself.
“In Poseidon’s name, you’re going the wrong way!”
He did not answer. There was no time. He found Teo behind the fallen rock.
“I’m so scared.”
“I got you. Run.”
They ran. Raken dragging Teo towards safety, stumbling, and behind the two of them the ceiling caved like a thing with a will and an appetite.
Ten paces from the light, Raken saw large, heavy, bone crushing boulders descending. Without thinking, he put both hands upon the boy and pushed forward and clear.
And the exit closed.
Chapter Three
It came down between them in one motion. Stone and crystal and gold all together, a wall where before then had been the way home.
He worked at escaping for a day, and a night, and another day. He shouted in anger. The rock took his voice and gave it back to him.
He took up his lamp, and he turned his back upon the only cave door he had ever known in all his life, and walked away from it into the unknown.
A mountain this old is not sealed shut. I have nothing else to do. I must find a way out.
He cut his marks upon the wall. One for every sleeping, in a neat row, like a man keeping accounts. For to keep the account is the same as believing that someone will one day read it.
On the sixth day he sat down upon the stone with his back against the wall.
I can’t believe I stood her up. She will be furious.
He laughed at the ceiling of a mountain that had swallowed him whole.
She will not weep, that one, and when I come out of here I shall not hear the end of it.
And Raken laughed out loud.
“And what can be so funny?”
He turned with the lamp, and there was a thing in the crevice with its legs braced against the stone, and it was the strangest sight of all his life.
“Forgive me. It is only that, from where I sit, you appear to be trapped for good.”
Broad and low and armored, the size of a fox, a shell across its back like an overturned dish, a fringe of small legs beneath it clicking on the rock. Two little stalks upon the front of it, both of them pointed straight at him, emanating a wisdom Raken had never known.
“What are you?”
“Older than your city. A great deal quieter. Which is how I heard you.”
Chapter Four
“You are hunting a door.”
“I am.”
“I have been the whole of this mountain, every crack of it, and I have had a long while to be thorough. There is air moving in a million places, and I have passed through all of them.”
“Then there is a way out.”
“Your spirit is strong, my young foolish one. The very best of your kind. Away outside, into the rain, wind and snow, the valleys and rivers. There are ways, sure. Yet for naught. People large, who came in at a hole, and have been calling it a door ever since.”
The creature went on, slowly clicking away into the dark.
Raken sat dejected, with his face in his hands, and did not move for a long while.
There was a world out there. There was air coming in from it, this very moment, across his own skin.
So he searched, for he was not yet a man who could think to stop. Determined to prove the creature wrong, he went deeper into the mountain than any man had ever gone. Than their fathers and their fathers before them, and he saw wonders that no one had ever seen.
The creature had told him true. There was no exit to be found.
Time came off in pieces. His accounts upon the wall went from neat rows, then to marks cut over the top of older marks, and then he stopped cutting them, because a man does not keep accounts for a creditor who is never coming.
He grew unkempt, wild. The bones came forward in his face until he was a different man altogether. He sat in the dark and whittled a flute out of a splinter of prop timber, because a man must do something with his hands, or else lose them.
And he was ready for the next dimension.
Out of nowhere, the mountain roared. The same voice as before. Low and long, rising and falling and rising again, like the growl of an enormous beast.
The ring of clouds above the summit wound tighter, three disks drawing in upon one another, faster and faster, the screw turning down into itself. And within the vapor a cold silver brightness rose up through it and passed and was gone.
The floor came up under his body and shook him hard. Rock let go above him. He rolled and covered his head, and the dust drifted down.
When it finally stopped, something felt different. He felt a pull. Something was calling.
Raken lifted his head. The roof had cracked. He knew every stone of that chamber by touch and by memory, and where there had been a wall there was now a shoulder of fallen rock.
He climbed far above, where in all his years nothing had ever been. A thread of silver light, beckoning.
He climbed higher. And higher. He got one hand upon a ledge and hung there with his arms shaking. And as his eyes came up over the edge, the whole of the opening ahead of him was full of moonlight.
And there, hanging from the wet lip of the rock, was something glowing. A shimmering white. Long strands of it, a full hand’s length, cascading down like the coat of some animal. Wet, and stirring in the air that came cool through.
It was the first living thing he had seen in that mountain that was not moss, and it was beautiful past all reason.
He reached out, extended. His mouth open and hungry.
It had come from nowhere, on the one ledge, hidden for an unknown amount of time. It had been waiting. He had arrived.
Chapter Five
His hand opened and closed, for his life depended on it. He ate.
And when he sat back against the stone, with the last of it upon his beard and upon the rock beside him, his eyes fell closed, and he let out one long breath that had been in him for years.
For the first time in longer than he could name, he was full, and it was such a small and ridiculous happiness that it very nearly undid him.
He slept where he sat, and he dreamed vibrant, and the dream was clear. It arrived, a vision, a manifestation of all things into form. He let go and accepted.
He dreamed of his room. The narrow bed, the weight of the pillow over his face, and the loud good dark beneath it.
He saw the crew running in terror. A worker running for his life, carrying his tool for cutting through hard rock, and the pick coming loose from his hand as great danger loomed. The tool striking the tunnel floor, bouncing once and lying still in the dust, while the man who had carried it ran on toward the light of survival.
Raken woke with his eyes wide, the way he had woken on the last ordinary morning of his life.
He was on his feet, and made haste. He ran. Running back along a road he had walked ten thousand times.
Was this dream of vision, or a cruel jest?
Half buried under a scatter of spoil, it was there. Where it had lain all the long years he had been slowly dying.
Iron. And a way out.
There is no telling how long it took him. Effort, and a will possessed by something greater than himself.
As the cold air caressed his face, the entire world opened. He was free.
Far along the shoulder of the second peak, he stood upon the snow and felt sorrow. A man, broken. No longer belonging to this land, or beyond.
He was white haired and white bearded and bent at the shoulder. There was not one thing left of the innocent young man, untested.
A hand came to his, and it closed hard around his fingers, and it did not let go.
“Better late than never.”
Their eyes met, and then Xiomara’s arms were about him, and his about her, and the two of them stood upon that mountainside, holding one another as though the universe had been made only that morning.
She too, older. There were years upon her face that had gone by without him, a life she had lived while he was under the ground. And he was older by three lifetimes and lighter by half his weight.
Neither of them cared.
Out upon the slope, a young man had stopped walking. He was not a child any more. He had a coil of rope over his shoulder and worn boots upon his feet and the long legs of somebody who had grown up walking that ground a long time. Searching.
And his face broke, and he smiled.
He came back for me.
And so I watched on in great relief as my sister and Raken embraced.
For many seasons I sought out the man I admired most. A debt, owed. For his age, his beard had grown so white and so long and unruly. From where I stood, it looked like a mane upon him.
