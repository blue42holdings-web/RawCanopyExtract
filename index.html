HERICIUM ERINACEUS · FRUITING BODY · DUAL EXTRACT

Lion's Mane

Grown in the dark. Made for the light.

WHAT IT IS

Hericium erinaceus grows as a white cascade of soft spines, hanging out of a wound in a hardwood — beech, oak, maple — like something poured and then frozen. It fruits in late summer and autumn across temperate forests in North America, Europe and East Asia, usually well off the ground, usually alone. Fresh, it has the texture of a sea creature; dried, it goes ivory and hard.

It has been gathered and prepared in East Asia for centuries, in kitchens as much as anywhere else, and it is one of the few mushrooms people recognise on sight without being told. Ours is cultivated on hardwood so the fruiting body can be harvested whole and extracted whole.

PART USED

Fruiting body only

A fruiting body is the mushroom itself. Mycelium is the root-like network it grows from, and most commercial "mushroom" products are mycelium grown on rice or oats, ground up together, so a large share of what's in the capsule is the grain it grew on. WHAT IT IS

Cordyceps militaris fruits as small orange clubs, a few centimetres tall, blunt at the tip and brighter than anything else on a forest floor. In the wild it is found across temperate Asia, Europe and North America. It is the cultivated relative of the high-altitude Himalayan species that has been collected by hand for generations and priced accordingly.

Ours is grown, not gathered — in trays, on a substrate the fruiting body is lifted off, so nothing but the mushroom goes into the extractor. Dried, it holds that orange; the powder is the colour of old brass.

Reishi

Out here, balance isn't a metaphor.

WHAT IT IS

Ganoderma lingzhi grows as a hard, kidney-shaped shelf off standing deadwood and stumps, banded in rust and mahogany with a surface that looks varnished by hand. It is woody, not edible in any ordinary sense, and it lasts — a shelf can hold its shape on a log through a whole winter.

It has been written about in East Asian materia medica for roughly two thousand years, always as something you prepare rather than something you eat. That is exactly what an extract is: the woody shelf, opened up by water and alcohol into something a cup can carry.

THE ROAR OF THE MOUNTAIN

A FICTIONAL ORIGIN STORY OF THE LION’S MANE MUSHROOM

In Orrin, the light comes out of the rock.

There is a mountain that wears a cloud, and the cloud does not leave it.

Three flat discs stacked one upon another, turning slow above the snow, winding down through the air like a screw that will never find its wood.

The wind comes off the high country and pours straight through it and out the far side and is gone, and still the cloud does not move, and it is not made of the same air twice in all a day. No one who lives beneath it has ever thought this strange. There has never been a morning without it.

The old people said the mountain was breathing. The young ones said it was only weather. Neither of them ever looked up long.

Inside the mountain it was morning also, though morning inside a mountain is a thing men agree upon rather than a thing they see.

High on the fourth terrace a woman leaned out over the rail and shook a blanket, and the dust came down through the crystal light in a long bright drift and took the better part of a minute to reach the floor.

Below her the great gallery of Orrin rose into the dark in terraces of cut stone — stairs folding back upon themselves, doors stacked six deep, bridges thrown across the gap at four heights, and above the highest terrace nothing at all, for no man had ever needed to know how far the ceiling went. Every foot of it lit by clear crystal set into the walls. Cold white light, and steady, and no flame anywhere in that world.

They were a people out of everywhere, for the country they had lost faced every direction and traded with all of it. Their grandfathers' grandfathers had come up out of the water with nothing but each other, and had found these caverns already cut and waiting, and had never learned by whom.

Their cooking smoke hung flat in a layer twenty feet up, having nowhere else to go, so that each morning the whole city lived beneath a ceiling of its own breakfast. A boy ran a bridge two levels above with a dog at his heels and the span rang like a struck pipe, and below him a man shouted at them both without troubling to look up.

This was the whole of the world, and it was enough, and no one there had ever wanted for more of it.

At the foot of the west stair the crew was waiting, and one of them had not come.

They stamped in the cold with their picks and their coiled rope and their crates stacked at their feet, and they looked up the stair, and they looked at one another, and no man said the thing that all of them were thinking, because all of them had said it before.

Then Adimu put his pick into Raj's hands.

Peace, he said. He is not lost. He is not hurt. He is asleep, as he was yesterday and as he will be tomorrow. I will fetch him.

And he went up, two steps at a time, and behind him somebody laughed, and the crew settled in to wait as men wait who have waited many times before.

Three flights up and along a passage was a room cut square from the rock, and in it a shelf, and a hook, and a coat upon the hook, and one crystal gone dark upon the shelf, and a narrow bed.

And upon the bed a man flat on his back with his legs thrown wide, one bare leg escaped from the sheet and hanging free, and a pillow pulled down over his eyes and both hands holding it fast against the morning.

Raken was snoring beneath it. Not politely. Wholeheartedly, with a rhythm to it and a catch at the top of every breath, and it filled the room entire, and it had likely been filling it for some hours.

Adimu stood in the doorway and looked upon him with great affection and no patience whatever.

Then he took the pillow from his face.

Raken came up as a man comes up out of deep water — eyes wide, hair flat upon the one side and standing upon the other, blinking at a room far brighter than he had agreed to. He was young and smooth of face, and his was the sort of face that has not yet been asked to do anything hard, and does not yet know that it will be.

Adimu was already at the door.

Up. And you may carry the box, sleepyhead. That is the price of a soft morning.

So he came out of his room with the crate in his arms and his coat half on and one boot not properly upon his foot, and he went down the last flights at a run.

And past the second landing there were women filling jars at the water channel, where the cold stream came down out of the rock and ran along the wall in its cut course, and one of them knelt at the edge of it with her sleeves shoved past the elbow and her forearms burned brown, and Xiomara looked back over her shoulder at him coming down the stair like a man falling downstairs on purpose.

They have you carrying boxes now, she said. How the mighty labor.

He stopped. He was very late, and he stopped anyway.

She rose to her feet, wiping her hands upon her skirts, waiting to see what he would make of that. Her two friends had gone quiet in the way that friends go quiet when they intend to hear everything.

What he did was bow to her. Deep, with the crate still in his arms, and no shame in him at all.

My lady. I had the finest evening of my life in your company, and I intend to have another. Tonight I mean to build upon it — trust first, then affection, then a house of our own, then a long and disgraceful old age with too many children in it.

You have thought about this a great deal for a man who cannot rise from his bed.

I have thought of nothing else. It is why I cannot rise from my bed.

Her friends were laughing. Xiomara did not laugh. She looked at him with the one eyebrow raised, which in her was the same as laughing, and he knew it, and she knew he knew it, and that was the whole of their courtship in a single look.

And upon the step above them, holding a bucket he had been sent to fill and had entirely forgotten, forgotten himself by every soul in that gallery, stood Teo. Nine years old. Her brother. Watching him the way a boy watches the only thing in the world he means to become.

Then Raken went — coat over the shoulder, a little skip, and he jumped and struck his heels together in the air and came down walking, and called back to her without ever turning his head.

I will see you tonight! I cannot wait for this day to be done!

And he was gone down the gallery.

And behind him the boy set his bucket upon the step. He slung his jacket over the one shoulder in the very same manner. He did the skip, and jumped, and struck his heels together — badly, landing hard, near going over — and grinned to himself, and ran after him into the dark of the passage.

Where do you think you are going? Xiomara on her feet with her hands wet, calling down the gallery after her brother. Come back here!

But the gallery was long, and the boy was quick, and there was water to be carried, and by the time she had turned back to her jar she had already decided he would be home by supper.

The working face was a raw place, and it was the whole of their inheritance.

Rough stone, timber props braced floor to ceiling, dust standing in the lamp beams, black water sheeting down one wall and running away in a cut channel. The air was wet and tasted of stone, and the sound of it was the sound of iron on rock, over and over, from four hundred years back to that morning.

For this was the labor of Orrin and the labor of their fathers before them: they made room.

The caverns were already there when the people came. No man knew who had cut the first of them. In the oldest galleries, where the walls ran smooth as poured glass and curved where no mason would think to curve them, no man had ever found the mark of a tool. But there were always more survivors than there was space, from the day the water took the old country to that very morning, and so the crews widened, gallery by gallery, life by life, cutting a city out of the dark a handspan at a time.

And the walls were full of wealth, and the wealth was nothing to them.

Gold lay in the seams in ropes and threads of it. Emerald showed green as deep water wherever the lamps swung by, and silver, and stones with no name that would have bought a kingdom on the surface of the earth. They shoveled all of it into the spoil and thought no more about it, for when a man has cut through gold every day of four hundred years, gold is gravel.

What they came for was the crystal. Clear as water, cold to the hand, glowing faint of its own accord in the shadow of a man's body. They cut it free and turned it once to check it and set it in the padded crates, and it went back into the city and into the walls, and the city stayed lit.

There was nothing fine about the work. It was hauling coal — save that what they hauled was the light that every soul they loved lived in.

Raken was bent to a seam with a wedge in his hand when the mountain spoke.

He had the crystal half free, the finest he had cut in a season, big as his fist and clean through as still water — and he stopped, and his face changed, for he had felt it before ever he heard it.

It was not a shake. Not yet. It was a buzz. Low and steady in the stone beneath his palm, the way a cold-box buzzes in the corner of a room where a man has long ago stopped hearing it.

He held still. He waited for it to be nothing.

And then it grew.

He turned and looked up the tunnel toward the mouth of it, and there was not one man standing in that whole length of rock. The dust was already coming down through the beams. The first stones were bouncing on the floor and skittering away into the dark, and the lamps hung on their nails were swinging all together, all in the same direction, like a thing agreed upon.

Then the roof let go.

Rock came off the ceiling in sheets. The props snapped one upon another down the whole run of them like a row of teeth. A dropped lamp rolled along the floor and threw its light spinning round the walls like something hunting for a way out, and men were running in every direction at once and shouting things that no one could hear.

And over all of it came Calder's voice, and Calder had his hands upon shoulders and was throwing men bodily toward the daylight.

Collapse! Everyone out! It is happening again!

Behind a fallen block, low to the ground, small, with his hands over his head and his face wet, was the boy.

He was making no sound whatever. There were clean tracks cut through the dust on his cheeks where the tears had run and gone on running, and his mouth was open, and nothing at all was coming out of it.

Raken saw him.

Where are you going?

Adimu roared it from the light, from safety, turned about with one hand flung out — for he had seen nothing, and there was nothing back there to see, and his friend was running the wrong way into a mountain that was coming down upon itself.

Raken! In God's name — where are you going?

He did not answer. There was no time and no breath and nothing he might have said that would have been worth the saying.

He went in.

He found the boy where he had fallen and got a hand about the small wrist.

I have you. I have you. Look at me — I have you.

And he hauled him up and he ran, and the boy ran with him, stumbling, half carried, and behind the two of them the ceiling came on after them like a thing with a will and an appetite.

Ten paces from the light he saw where the roof was going.

He did not stop to reckon it. He put both hands upon the boy and threw him — sideways, forward, clear — the way a man shoves a boat off a bank.

And then the mountain closed.

It came down between them in one motion, stone and crystal and gold all together, a wall where a breath before there had been a way home, and the sound of it went on rolling long after the last of it had fallen.

He worked at it for a day, and a night, and another day.

He hauled stone until his knuckles split and then he hauled with them split, and the blood made paste of the dust upon his hands. Some of those boulders were larger than he was. He set his shoulder beneath one and drove with his legs until his sight went white at the edges and it did not move the width of a finger. He took up a broken prop for a lever and the prop snapped in his grip and dropped him on his back in the rubble.

He shouted. There was no echo in that place. The rock took his voice and kept it and gave him nothing back.

And at the last he stood away from the face of it and looked at what it truly was.

This will not move, he said, to no one at all. Not by these hands. Not by any hands that ever were. There is no strength in me that answers this.

He took up his lamp, and turned his back upon the only door he had ever known in all his life, and walked away from it into the dark.

Then I shall find another. A mountain this old is not sealed shut. Somewhere there is another way, and I have nothing else to do but find it.

He cut his marks upon the wall, one for every sleeping, in a neat row like a man keeping accounts — for to keep the account is the same as believing that someone will one day read it.

On the fifth day he was cutting the fifth mark when the thought took him sideways, and he sat down upon the stone with his back against the wall and laughed out loud.

Oh. Oh, the date. She will be furious.

He laughed at the ceiling of a mountain that had swallowed him whole.

She will not weep, that one. She will not fear for me. She will stand at the bottom of the west stair with her arms folded and she will decide that I overslept — and she will be right in every particular save the only one that matters, and when I come out of here I shall not hear the end of it for thirty years.

And what, said a voice, is so funny to you?

He had not heard it with his ears. It had arrived in the middle of his own head, mild as a neighbor over a fence.

Forgive me. It is only that from where I sit, you appear to be trapped.

He turned with the lamp, and there was a thing in the crevice with its legs braced against the stone, and it was the strangest sight of all his life.

Broad and low and armored, the size of a hound. A shell across its back like an overturned dish, ridged and pearled at the edge as a shell is when it has grown in water. A fringe of small legs beneath it, clicking on the rock. Two little stalks upon the front of it, both of them pointed straight at him, and both of them trembling.

It was a thing that belonged upon the floor of an ocean. It looked, in truth, like something that had been upon the floor of an ocean once, and had come here as his own people had come here, and had simply never left.

What are you? Raken said.

Older than your city, and a great deal quieter. Which is how I heard you. The stalks steadied by degrees. You are hunting a door.

I am.

Then I shall spare you the years. And there was no cruelty in it anywhere. It was the kindest voice he had ever heard. I have been the whole of this mountain. Every crack of it. Every seam. Every drop of water in it, and I have had a long while to be thorough. There is air moving in a dozen places and I have gone through most of them.

Then there is a way out.

There are ways. A pause, and the stalks turned toward the dark, and then back. Not for the newcomers. Not for you people, who came in at a hole and have been calling it a door ever since.

And then, gently, as a man lays a stone upon a grave:

Not at your size.

The creature went, in its own time, clicking away into the dark and taking no offense at anything, and left him with it.

And Raken sat with his face in both his hands and did not move for a long while, for the thing had gone into him the way a roof goes into a house.

There was a world out there. There was air coming in from it, this very moment, across his own skin. And the mountain would let a thing the size of a dish walk out through the seams of it and would not let him.

Still he searched, for he was not yet a man who could stop.

And in the searching he went deeper into that mountain than any man of his crew had ever gone, deeper than their fathers and their fathers before them, and he saw what no one of Orrin had ever seen.

He found a hall of white stone with a black lake lying in the floor of it, so utterly still that his one small lamp burned twice, once in the air and once beneath, and he stood at the edge of it a long time and did not dare to touch the water.

He found a gallery hung with columns grown down from the ceiling and up from the floor to meet in the middle, over a stretch of time he had no number large enough for.

He found a chamber of crystal so thick and so clear that when he stood at the center of it and held up his flame, the light went on in every direction forever, and did not stop, and he wept there — alone, an ordinary man, in a room more beautiful than anything his people had ever built, that no one else would ever see.

But the creature had told him true. He walked and he climbed and he crawled and he counted his paces in the dark, and there was no door.

Time came off him then in pieces.

The lamp died and did not come again. The marks upon the wall went from one neat row to three, and then to marks cut over the top of older marks, and then he stopped cutting them, because a man does not keep accounts for a creditor who is never coming.

He learned the dark as a man learns a strange house — the drip that means water, the four paces to the wall, the eleven to the corner. He ate what grew on the wet walls and what moved in it, and he grew patient in a way that would have frightened his mother.

His coat went short, cut away in strips to bind his feet when his boots gave out. His hands went hard and then thin. The beard came in and thickened and grayed. His hair went long down his back. The bones came forward in his face until it was a different face.

And at the last, when there was nothing left to search and nothing left to hope for, he sat in the dark and whittled a flute out of a splinter of prop-timber, because a man must do something with his hands or else lose them.

What was it that I did? he asked the dark, turning the flute over and over. I have gone back through all of it a hundred times and I cannot find the sin. I was late to my work. I bowed to a girl in the morning. I went back for a child — and I would go back again tomorrow, and again the day after that, and you may put me here again for it and I would still go.

He blew across the reed and it gave a poor thin note that went nowhere.

So there is no sin, and there is no reason, and it is only what happened. Very well. Then this is my life, and I have had it, and this is the rest of it.

And he was ready to be done.

And the mountain roared.

The same voice as before. Low and long, rising and falling and rising again, the growl of some enormous beast turning over in the deep beneath him. The floor came up under his body. Rock let go above him in the black and he rolled and covered his head, and stone came down four feet from where he lay, and then it stopped, and the dust drifted down, and there was nothing at all.

And upon the outside of the mountain, in the full moon, the snow was moving.

The cloud above the summit wound tighter — the three discs drawing in upon one another, faster and faster, the screw turning down into itself — and within the vapor a cold silver brightness rose up through it and passed and was gone.

Then it slackened. Opened out. And returned to its old slow turning, exactly as it had always been.

No one saw it. There was no one there to see. There never has been.

Inside, in the dark, Raken lifted his head.

The roof had changed.

He knew every stone of that chamber by touch and by memory, and where there had been a wall there was now a shoulder of fallen rock heaped against it and reaching upward — and far above it, where in all his years nothing had ever been, a thread of silver light.

He climbed.

On bound feet, with old hands that had forgotten the trick of it, he climbed higher than he had ever been able to climb in that place, and then higher, and got one hand upon a ledge and hung there with his arms shaking, and hauled.

And as his eyes came up over the edge, the whole of the opening ahead of him was full of moonlight.

And there, hanging from the wet lip of the rock, was something white.

Long strands of it, a full hand's length, cascading down like the coat of some animal, wet and shining and stirring in the air that came through. It was the first living thing he had seen in that mountain that was not moss and was not water, and it was beautiful past all reason.

He got himself onto the ledge and he stared at it with his mouth open like a boy.

What are you? he said. What in the world are you — growing here, in this place, where there is no reason for anything at all?

It did not answer him, being a thing that grows on rock. But he sat on that ledge in the moonlight and he looked at it, and he could not stop looking at it.

He had cut a mountain apart with his hands. He had walked halls of white stone and stood in a room of light and seen wonders his whole people would never know of. And not one of them had struck him the way this struck him — this small extravagant white thing, hanging in the cold off a wet lip of rock, a hand's breadth of impossible life in a place with no soil and no sun and no cause.

It had come from nowhere. It had grown for no one. It had waited, in the dark, on the one ledge in that mountain he had never in all his years been able to reach.

And now here he was.

Well, he said at last. We are both of us in the wrong place, and neither of us was asked.

Beside it there was a gap in the rock, and the cold was pouring through it, and far away past it, black against a blue field of snow, the shapes of trees.

He put his arm into that gap up to the shoulder.

His hand opened and closed upon the open air of the world — upon the outside, upon the very night — and there was nothing there to take hold of, and the gap was two fingers wide, and it would not have him.

He drew back and put his brow against the stone.

No. Not this way. Not without iron. A man would need a pick and a month of days to open this, and I have neither, and have had neither for longer than I am able to count.

The wind came through the gap and moved his hair, and out there beyond his reach the trees went on standing in the moon, as they had every night that he had been buried.

And then, because he was starving, and because it was the only living thing within his reach in all the world, he lifted the same arm and closed his hand upon the white strands and pulled them free.

He ate it there on the ledge in the moonlight, and he did not save any of it, and he did not think twice.

And when it was done he sat back against the stone with the last of it upon his beard and upon the rock beside him, and his eyes half closed, and he let out one long breath that had been in him for years.

He was full. For the first time in longer than he could name, he was simply full — and it was such a small and ridiculous happiness that it very nearly undid him.

He slept where he sat. Head back, mouth open, snoring like a man in his own bed on a soft morning with somewhere to be.

And he dreamed, and the dream was clearer than anything he had seen with his eyes in years.

He dreamed his own room. The shelf and the hook and the coat upon the hook. The narrow bed. The weight of the pillow over his face and the loud good dark beneath it, and the sudden white flood of the world when it was pulled away, and Adimu standing over him with no patience whatever.

He dreamed the crew ahead of him in the tunnel with the dust coming down, and Calder among them with his hands upon their shoulders, driving them at the daylight, shouting.

And he dreamed Raj — running past him, before the roof came down, before any of it — with a pick over his shoulder.

And the pick coming loose.

And the pick striking the tunnel floor and bouncing once and lying still in the dust, while the man who had carried it ran on toward the light and never looked back for it.

Raken woke with his eyes wide, the way he had woken on the last ordinary morning of his life.

It fell inside.

He was on his feet before he had finished the words, and he said them again to the dark, out loud, to nobody, because there was no one to say them to and he could not hold them in his mouth.

He dropped it on this side of the wall. Before the roof came down — I saw it fall and I stepped over it and I have not thought of it once from that hour to this. It is on my side. It has been on my side the whole time.

And he ran.

An old man now, white-haired, wrapped in rags, running through a mountain he had given himself up to, running back along a road he had walked ten thousand times in the dark toward a place he had passed a thousand times without stopping.

It was there.

Half buried under a scatter of spoil with the haft of it showing, exactly where it had come to rest — where it had lain in the dark all the long years he had been dying twenty paces away from it.

Iron. And a good haft. And a way out.

He got down on his knees in the rubble and put both hands on it, and for a while he did not do anything else at all.

What came after was work, and he knew work.

He went back to the gap where the white thing had grown and he set the iron against the rock, and he swung.

There is no telling how long it took him. He was not counting any more. He struck and he rested and he struck again, and he ate what the walls gave him, and he slept at the foot of his own labor, and he woke and he swung. The gap gave a finger's width, and then a hand's, and then it took the whole of his arm, and then his head and shoulders, and he crawled and struck and crawled again, and there was cold air on his face the entire way and it never once stopped coming.

And then it was not the same mountain any longer. The stone changed under his hands. It went smooth and it went curved, poured-looking, dressed by no chisel that any man of Orrin had ever swung, and he crawled through galleries that had been cut by somebody before his people had ever come up out of the sea — and he did not stop to wonder at them, not then, because there was moving air ahead and he was following it.

He came up and around and out, far along the shoulder of the mountain, under the second peak — that is how far he had gone inside, and how deep, and how long.

And he came out into the open air of the world, and stood upon the snow, with the pick still in his hand.

He was white-haired and white-bearded and bent at the shoulder, and the beard came down his chest, and there was not one thing left of the young man who had been late for work.

The sky was enormous. He had forgotten that. He had to sit down on a rock for a while before he could stand up under it.

And when he could stand, he turned and looked back along the whole vast flank of the mountain to the great peak beyond, and at the three discs of cloud turning slowly above it — turning as they had turned on the morning he went in, and on every morning since, and on every morning he had lain in the dark under them.

He looked at it a long time.

And am I still myself? he said. Or did I leave him in there? I have come out of a mountain, and I do not know what it is that has come out.

A hand came into his.

Small, and sunburned, and known to him — known before he had even turned his head, known the way a man knows his own name in a crowd — and it closed hard about his fingers, and it did not let go.

And then her arms were about him, and his about her, and the two of them stood upon that mountainside holding one another as though the world had been made only that morning.

She was gray at the temple now. There were years upon her face that had gone by without him, whole seasons of them, a life she had lived while he was under the ground. And he was older by three lifetimes and lighter by half his weight and his beard was in her hair.

Neither of them cared one particle about any of it.

She had not wept when they told her he was gone. She had not wept at the first burial nor at the second. She wept now, and he held on to her, and neither one of them said anything at all, because there was nothing that needed saying and there never had been.

And out upon the slope, a way off, a young man had stopped walking.

He was not a child any more. He had a coil of rope over his shoulder and worn boots upon his feet and the long legs of somebody who had grown up while nobody was watching, and he had been walking that ground a long time — that same ground, that same slope, season after season, since he was a boy of nine with a bucket he had been sent to fill.

He stood, and he watched the two of them.

And his face broke, and he smiled.

He came back for me.

He was down there so long that we buried him twice. Once when the mountain closed, and once again years after, when we had run out of ways to keep saying he was coming.

I watched him come down off the second peak. He had gone white in there — every hair of him — and his beard had grown out so long and so wild that from where I stood, it looked like a mane upon him.
